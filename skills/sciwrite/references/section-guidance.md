# Section guidance

Read this file in **full-review** and **section-review** mode, and apply the
part for each section under review. It covers what each section of a research
article must do, so that Pass 3 can judge structure at the level of the
section as well as the paragraph. Abstracts are covered by
`summary-paragraph.md`, not here.

Report these findings under Pass 3. Calibrate them like any other finding: a
section that omits something the reader needs is MAJOR; an ordering or tense
problem is MINOR. Journals and fields differ (some merge Results and
Discussion, some put Methods last); follow the manuscript's own structure and
judge each part by what it is meant to do.

## Introduction

The Introduction is a funnel: from what is known, to what is not known, to
what this paper does about it. It is usually short, often three to five
paragraphs.

- **Known:** opens with the broad context the reader needs, supported by
  citations. Flag background that runs on for paragraphs before reaching the
  problem.
- **Gap:** states clearly what is unknown, unresolved or wrong in previous
  work. This is the sentence that justifies the paper. If the gap is implied
  but never stated, MAJOR.
- **This paper:** ends with what the authors did and, if the field expects it,
  the main finding ("Here we show…"). If the Introduction ends without saying
  what this paper does, MAJOR.
- Leave detailed methods and results out of the Introduction.

## Methods

The Methods let a competent reader repeat the work.

- Group by subheadings or by step, in a logical order (often the order of the
  Results).
- Cite established methods instead of re-describing them; describe only what is
  new or changed.
- Past tense for what was done. Passive voice is acceptable here when the actor
  doesn't matter (see Pass 2).
- Flag interpretation or results that have crept into the Methods, and
  background that belongs in the Introduction.
- Do not ask for more detail on content grounds; that is the reviewers' job. Do
  flag a step that is mentioned but never explained when the text itself
  depends on it.

## Results

The Results report what was found, in order, without interpreting it.

- **Lead with the finding, not the figure.** "Figure 3 shows the drag
  coefficient for each surface…" makes the reader look for the point. Write the
  finding and cite the figure in parentheses: "The riblet surface reduced drag
  by 7% (Figure 3)."
  MINOR, or MAJOR when a paragraph never states its finding.
- **No interpretation.** Explanations of why a result occurred, and comparisons
  with other studies, belong in the Discussion. Flag them and say so. MINOR,
  unless the interpretation is presented as a finding.
- **Don't repeat every number from the tables.** Give the key values in the
  text and point to the table for the rest.
- Past tense for the authors' own findings ("drag fell by 7%").

## Discussion

The Discussion is the funnel inverted: from this paper's answer, back out to
what it means for the field.

- **Open with the main finding,** stated as the answer to the question posed in
  the Introduction. A Discussion that opens by restating the methods or listing
  results is MAJOR.
- **Don't repeat the Results.** Refer to findings briefly; do not restate
  numbers at length.
- **Compare with previous work,** saying where results agree, disagree, and
  why.
- **Limitations:** a Discussion with none is missing something reviewers will
  ask for. Note it; MAJOR in a full review.
- **Implications and a take-home message:** end with what the reader should
  remember, not with a new result or a future-work list.
- **Overreach is a content note, not an edit.** Words such as "prove",
  "optimal", "all", "always" or "first" often claim more than the results
  show. Flag the claim under Content notes and offer a measured alternative as
  an option, but never weaken or strengthen it in a revision on your own (see
  Gotchas).

## Tense across sections

Use the present tense for established knowledge and for what the paper itself
contains ("Figure 2 shows", "Section 3 describes"); the past tense for what the
authors did and found. Flag a switch only when it misleads, for example the
authors' new result stated in the present tense as if it were settled
knowledge.
